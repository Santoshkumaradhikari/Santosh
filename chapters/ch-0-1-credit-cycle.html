Nepal’s Credit Cycle: The Hidden Equity Cycle

First published 21 Aug 2026 · Last verified 31 Aug 2026
Scope note: This chapter presents an evidence-informed framework, not a mechanical trading rule. The relationships below are hypotheses to test against current NRB, NEPSE and company data. They are not forecasts or investment recommendations.
In this chapter
  1. Credit growth and NEPSE: association, mechanism and limits
  2. Remittance → deposits → credit
  3. Credit, bank earnings and the lag problem
  4. Credit contraction, asset quality and market risk
  5. A three-number starting dashboard
Core thesis: Credit and liquidity are important common drivers of Nepal’s equity market. NRB strongly influences the monetary and regulatory environment, but transmission also depends on borrower demand, bank behaviour, asset quality, remittances, economic activity, valuation and investor expectations.

0.1.1 — Credit growth and NEPSE: association, mechanism and limits

A share price reflects company-specific information, valuation, earnings expectations and market liquidity. In Nepal, banks, regulation, remittances and interest rates are especially important parts of that environment. The useful question is not whether credit growth always makes stocks rise. It is through which channels, with what delay, and under what conditions credit affects bank earnings, company cash flows and equity valuations.

NRB policy and liquidity operations
              ↓
Funding conditions, deposits and bank incentives
              ↓
Private-sector credit, rates and allocation
              ↓
Household/business activity and bank asset quality
              ↓
Earnings expectations, risk appetite and valuation
              ↓
NEPSE prices and turnover

Why the relationship is plausible

  1. Financial-sector weight: banks and other financial institutions have historically represented a large share of listed-market capitalisation and turnover, although hydropower and other sectors have grown.
  2. Bank-centred financing: households and businesses depend on bank credit for property, working capital and investment.
  3. Market concentration: limited formal hedging and a large retail presence can make liquidity and risk appetite important during advances and corrections.
  4. Collateral feedback: lending against securities can amplify moves when falling collateral values trigger calls or deleveraging.

What evidence must show

Define the credit series, market return series, frequency, lags and control variables. Test policy rates, inflation, remittances, reserves, GDP, turnover, political events and global risk. Use returns and changes as well as levels: two trending series can produce spurious correlation. “Association”, “transmission channel” and “testable causal hypothesis” are safer descriptions than claiming that credit explains most market movement.

0.1.2 — Remittance → deposits → credit

Remittances are a major source of foreign exchange and household income. They can support deposits, but a remittance receipt does not automatically become a new loan: it may be consumed, used to repay debt, invested in land, retained as a deposit or transferred through another channel.

Foreign employment and remittance receipts
              ↓
Household income, consumption, repayment and saving
              ↓
Deposits and foreign-exchange reserves
              ↓
Bank liquidity, funding mix and risk appetite
              ↓
Credit supply × borrower demand
              ↓
Economic activity, earnings and sentiment

CRR and SLR constrain liquidity, but they do not determine lending capacity by themselves. Capital adequacy, CD-ratio rules, liquidity, borrower demand, underwriting, loan pricing, sector limits, NPLs and provisioning also matter. It is misleading to claim that NPR 100 of deposits mechanically produces a fixed NPR 80–86 of lending.

Correct rule: reserve and liquidity rules constrain deployment of deposits, but new credit is an outcome of regulation, capital, liquidity, bank behaviour and demand—not a fixed money-multiplier percentage.

0.1.3 — Credit, bank earnings and the lag problem

Credit does not translate into EPS on a fixed timetable. A possible sequence is policy change, bank repricing and underwriting, loan disbursement, interest-income and funding-cost changes, provisions and recoveries, quarterly reporting, and investor expectation revisions.

Analyse loan growth and mix, lending yields, deposit costs, NIM or net interest spread, CASA and term deposits, fees, treasury income, operating costs, gross and net NPLs, provision coverage, recoveries, write-offs, capital adequacy and regulatory changes. A policy-rate lag is not a universal EPS lag for every bank.

0.1.4 — Credit contraction, asset quality and market risk

Rapid credit growth can increase later asset-quality risk when underwriting weakens, collateral prices outrun income, or lending concentrates in cyclical sectors. NPL deterioration is not inevitable and is not caused by tightening alone.

Tighter conditions or weaker activity
              ↓
Debt service becomes harder for some borrowers
              ↓
Overdue loans, restructuring and NPL classification
              ↓
Provisioning, recoveries and capital pressure
              ↓
Profit or ROE pressure; possible valuation de-rating
              ↓
Bank and market repricing

Do not apply a universal “one percentage point NPL increase equals X percentage points of provisions” rule. Required provisions depend on classification, collateral, existing coverage, recoveries, write-offs and NRB rules.

The 2021–2022 correction is a useful case study, but it should be documented with dated NEPSE levels, credit, deposits, CD ratio, interest rates, margin-loan rules, bank NPLs and the dates information became public. NRB’s 2021/22 policy introduced a maximum 90% CD-ratio requirement and later policy documents addressed lending against share collateral; these are sourceable events, not proof that one variable caused the entire correction.

0.1.5 — A three-number starting dashboard

Three indicators are a useful monthly entry point, not a complete model.

  1. Private-sector credit growth: record the exact definition, frequency and reporting date; separate nominal from real growth.
  2. CD ratio: identify whether it covers commercial banks, all BFIs or one institution. A regulatory ceiling is not a system average.
  3. Weighted-average interbank rate: compare it with the relevant policy corridor and observe persistence, not one-day spikes.

Confirm the picture with deposits, remittances, lending and deposit rates, inflation, reserves, credit-to-GDP, NPLs, provisions, capital, sector allocation, valuation, turnover, breadth and margin-loan data where reliable.

Conclusion

Nepal’s credit cycle is a valuable lens because it connects NRB policy, deposits, lending, bank earnings, household balance sheets and equity-market liquidity. It is not a single-variable explanation of NEPSE or a guaranteed timing system. Define the data, read current releases, separate liquidity from demand, distinguish association from causality, test lags and asset quality, compare macro signals with valuation, and record what would prove the framework wrong.

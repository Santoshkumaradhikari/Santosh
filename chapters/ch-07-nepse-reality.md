> *Before any strategy, instrument, or ratio — understand the arena you are actually in.*

Most investors who lose money in Nepal's stock market do not lose it because they chose the wrong stock. They lose it because they applied the wrong mental model to the right stock in a market they did not truly understand. They treated NEPSE like a shrunken version of the New York Stock Exchange, or perhaps like a local analogue of India's BSE or NSE. They imported frameworks — strict P/E ratio screening, MACD crossovers, institutional momentum strategies — that were forged in markets of entirely different character, depth, and institutional architecture.

This chapter is not a warning against analysis. It is a foundation for it. Before you open a balance sheet, before you calculate a price-to-book ratio, before you chart a moving average, you must first understand the arena you have chosen to enter. What kind of market is NEPSE? Who built it, who owns it, who moves it, and why does it sometimes behave in ways that seem to defy both logic and global trends? These are not peripheral questions. They are the first questions — and the answers to them will shape every investment decision you make.

## Lesson 7.1 — What a Stock Exchange Actually Is: Ownership, Price Discovery, and Capital Formation

### The Foundational Concept

A stock exchange is, at its simplest, an organised marketplace where buyers and sellers of financial securities come together to transact. But this simple definition conceals three functions so fundamental that failure to understand any one of them leads to profound misunderstanding of markets as a whole. Those three functions are: **the creation and transfer of ownership**, **the mechanism of price discovery**, and **the process of capital formation**.

#### Ownership: What You Are Actually Buying

When you purchase shares of a company listed on NEPSE — say, Nepal Investment Mega Bank (NIMB) or Nabil Bank — you are not lending money to that company. You are not purchasing a bond with a guaranteed return. You are acquiring partial ownership of the enterprise. You become, legally and economically, a shareholder: a fractional owner of everything the company owns, net of everything it owes.

This ownership carries specific rights under the Nepal Companies Act, 2063 (2006). You have the right to vote at Annual General Meetings (AGMs) on matters of corporate governance — the election of directors, approval of audited financial statements, appointment of statutory auditors, and proposed amendments to the company's Memorandum and Articles of Association (MoA/AoA). You have the right to receive dividends, if and when the board of directors proposes and the AGM approves them. And you have the right to receive a proportional share of whatever value remains if the company is liquidated — though this residual claim ranks last behind secured creditors, bondholders, depositors, and government tax obligations.

This is a critical distinction. A depositor at a commercial bank is a creditor of that bank. If the bank encounters insolvency, the depositor has a prior claim on assets (supplemented by deposit insurance schemes through the Deposit and Credit Guarantee Fund up to statutory thresholds). A shareholder of that same bank is an owner. If the bank fails catastrophically, the shareholder may recover nothing. The potential upside of ownership — capital appreciation, cash dividends, and bonus shares (*right and bonus issuances*) — is fundamentally paired with the downside of complete capital loss. Understanding this is not pessimism; it is the prerequisite for rational risk pricing.

#### Price Discovery: The Continuous Auction

Markets discover prices. Prior to organised exchanges, determining the fair value of a company's equity stake required private negotiation — an opaque, illiquid, and inherently inefficient process. The exchange changes this by creating a continuous, transparent double-auction mechanism.

On NEPSE, during official trading hours (**Sunday through Thursday, 11:00 AM to 3:00 PM Nepal Standard Time**, preceded by a pre-open order collection session from 10:30 AM to 10:45 AM), thousands of individual orders enter the NEPSE Automated Trading System (NATS) via registered broker Trading Management Systems (TMS). The matching engine executes these orders strictly on **price-time priority**. The price at which the last transaction executes is broadcast in real time as the Last Traded Price (LTP). This is the discovered market price: not what any single participant thinks the company should be worth, but the price at which a willing buyer and a willing seller actually agreed to transact.

Price discovery is valuable to society beyond its utility to individual traders. It aggregates dispersed information — the collective knowledge, expectations, liquidity needs, and risk assessments of thousands of participants — into a single number. When a commercial bank discloses robust quarterly earnings or improves its Non-Performing Loan (NPL) ratio, buyers step in, bidding prices upward. When a run-of-river hydropower company faces dry-season hydrology drops or transmission line bottlenecks, sellers exit, driving prices downward. Prices encode information. This is the core mechanism of market efficiency — not perfect efficiency, but information-responsiveness over time.

In NEPSE's case, price discovery is meaningful but structurally imperfect. Thin trading liquidity in select counters, retail-dominated participation, informal broker syndicates, and structural information asymmetries mean that NEPSE prices often reflect short-term rumor and liquidity conditions rather than intrinsic value, sometimes lagging fundamental corporate realities by months.

#### Capital Formation: The Economic Justification

The third function of a stock exchange — capital formation — is its primary contribution to national economic development. A stock exchange allows enterprises to mobilize capital from the public by issuing shares through an Initial Public Offering (IPO), Further Public Offering (FPO), or Rights Issue. In exchange for this equity capital, the issuing company grants investors ownership stakes and voting rights.

Equity financing provides a resilient alternative to debt financing. A company that funds its expansion exclusively through bank loans must service interest and principal repayments regardless of macroeconomic conditions — a fixed contractual obligation that creates severe fragility during economic downturns. In contrast, an enterprise funded via equity has no contractual repayment obligation. Its shareholders absorb business risk, and if the venture prospers, they participate in the value created.

For Nepal, capital formation through NEPSE has been pivotal in building the modern banking, insurance, and energy sectors. NEPSE has been the primary vehicle through which millions of Nepali citizens have participated in the equity financing of commercial banks, development banks, microfinance institutions, and dozens of privately developed hydropower projects across the nation's river basins. 

Regulatory provisions set by the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON) — including the mandatory minimum 10-kitta allotment policy, the reservation of 10% of IPOs for Nepalis employed abroad under formal remittance channels, and 10% allocations for project-affected local residents in infrastructure and hydropower — have driven widespread retail participation unprecedented for an economy at Nepal's per-capita income level.

#### Key Concepts in Lesson 7.1
- **Shareholders are residual owners, not creditors** — upside is uncapped, but equity losses can be total.
- **Price discovery aggregates dispersed knowledge** into a live market clearing price based on price-time priority.
- **Trading hours run Sunday through Thursday, 11:00 AM to 3:00 PM NST** (Pre-open: 10:30 AM – 10:45 AM).
- **Capital formation via equity** allows national infrastructure (especially hydropower and financial institutions) to mobilize long-term risk capital without fixed debt service fragility.
- **SEBON's allocation quotas** (10-kitta rule, remittance quota, local resident quota) have structurally broadened retail access.

## Lesson 7.2 — The History of NEPSE: From 1993 to Today — How the Market Was Built and What Shaped It

### Origins: Securities Exchange Centre (SEC), 1976

NEPSE's institutional ancestor, the **Securities Exchange Centre (SEC)**, was established in 1976 under the Companies Act of 1964 with the joint initiative of Nepal Rastra Bank and the Nepal Industrial Development Corporation (NIDC). The SEC was a state body that performed multiple simultaneous roles: stock broker, market maker, issue manager, and share registry administrator. By modern governance standards, this concentration of functions was heavily conflicted. Transactions were largely manual, Over-The-Counter (OTC), and based on informal bilateral negotiations between buyers and sellers.

Securities traded were few: government treasury bonds, national development bonds, and a handful of shares in public utilities and state-sponsored financial institutions. Trading volume was negligible. Nepal's economy in the 1970s and 1980s was predominantly agrarian, formal financial intermediation was low, and private corporate enterprise was in its infancy.

### The Birth of NEPSE: 1993–1994

In line with macroeconomic liberalization policies supported by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank in the early 1990s, the Government of Nepal restructured the capital market framework. Under the Securities Exchange Act, 2040 (1983, First Amendment 1993), the Securities Exchange Centre was formally converted into the **Nepal Stock Exchange Limited (NEPSE)**, incorporated on **January 13, 1993**. 

NEPSE officially commenced open-outcry trading on **January 16, 1994**, with the benchmark **NEPSE Index established with a base value of 100 on February 12, 1994 (Magh 30, 2050 BS)**. Simultaneously, the **Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON)** was established in 1993 as the apex capital market regulator.

### The 1990s and 2000s: Paper Shares to Institutional Expansion

Through the late 1990s, NEPSE developed slowly. Physical share certificates, paper transfer deeds, manual clearing, and slow endorsement processes created long transaction cycles (often taking weeks or months to complete title transfers) and persistent settlement risk. The 1996–2006 internal conflict and political instability suppressed private sector listings outside the financial sector.

The mid-2000s brought rapid financial sector deregulation under Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB). Liberal licensing policies led to a surge in commercial banks, development banks, and finance companies. Because regulatory mandates required licensed financial institutions to issue public equity within a specified operational window, NEPSE's listed universe became heavily weighted toward banking and financial institutions (BFIs).

### The Modernisation Era: CDS and Clearing (CDSC) & Electronic Trading

To eliminate paper certificate risks, **CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC)** was incorporated in 2010 as a wholly-owned subsidiary of NEPSE. The introduction of dematerialized (Demat) securities and electronic book-entry settlement transformed the market. By 2015–2016, dematerialized trading became fully mandatory across all listed equity. In late 2018, NEPSE launched its web-based **Trade Management System (TMS)**, allowing retail investors to place orders directly online through licensed broker terminals and verify balances via the **MeroShare** portal.

### The 2015 Earthquake and Market Decoupling

The devastating 7.8-magnitude Gorkha Earthquake of April 2015 killed nearly 9,000 people and inflicted over $7 billion in physical and economic losses (roughly one-third of Nepal's GDP at the time). Yet, when trading resumed after an initial temporary suspension, the NEPSE index demonstrated remarkable resilience and soon began a multi-year bull market.

This apparent paradox highlighted a structural reality: **NEPSE is not an index of the physical real economy.** The earthquake severely damaged subsistence agriculture, rural housing, and informal small businesses — sectors entirely absent from the stock exchange. The listed universe — dominated by commercial banks, insurance firms, and urban financial institutions — remained solvent. Post-disaster reconstruction spending, substantial foreign aid inflows, and resilient remittance transfers increased financial system liquidity, which directly fueled equity market expansion.

## Lesson 7.3 — NEPSE vs. BSE, NSE, NYSE: Scale, Depth, and Why Comparisons Must Be Made Carefully

### Why Comparisons Are Both Necessary and Dangerous

Investors who study global market literature — whether Benjamin Graham's value principles on Wall Street or institutional trading on the National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) — frequently attempt to transplant those exact analytical models into NEPSE. While core arithmetic remains universal (cash flows, book values, earnings yields), the operational and structural rules of the marketplace differ drastically.

### Structural Comparison Table

| Metric | NYSE (USA) | BSE / NSE (India) | NEPSE (Nepal) | Analytical Implication for NEPSE |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Listed Securities** | ~2,400+ | ~2,200 (NSE) / ~5,400 (BSE) | ~270+ scrips (~250+ equity companies) | Narrower investment universe; high sector concentration. |
| **Market Capitalisation** | ~$28–30+ Trillion | ~$4.5–5.0+ Trillion | ~$25–36 Billion (NPR 3.5–4.8 Trillion) | Small domestic market; highly sensitive to central bank policy. |
| **Average Daily Turnover** | ~$20–50+ Billion | ~$8–12 Billion (Cash) | ~$30–225 Million (NPR 4–29.95 Billion) | Lower liquidity; large transactions induce significant price impact. |
| **Benchmark Index** | S&P 500 / DJIA | NIFTY 50 / SENSEX | NEPSE Index | All-inclusive cap-weighted index rather than a curated blue-chip basket. |
| **Derivatives Market** | Deep & liquid (Options/Futures) | Massive index & equity derivatives market | Absent / Unregulated | No exchange-traded put options to hedge portfolio downside. |
| **Market Participant Base** | ~75–80% Institutional | ~65–70% Institutional | ~80–85% Retail | High emotional volatility; prolonged momentum and panic cycles. |
| **Settlement Cycle** | T+1 | T+1 (with optional T+0) | T+2 | Standard CDSC clearing cycle requires 2 business days. |
| **Short Selling** | Permitted & regulated | Permitted & regulated | **Not permitted** | Market only provides one-way downward adjustment via direct selling. |
| **Circuit Breakers** | Market-wide index triggers (7%, 13%, 20%) | Market-wide & stock price bands | **Market-wide (4%, 5%, 6%) + Individual stock (±10%)** | Tight volatility halts limit intraday single-stock price swings. |

### Scale: The Liquidity Impact

In deep global markets, an institutional investor can accumulate or liquidate millions of dollars in a blue-chip company without significantly distorting the market price. The presence of market makers, algorithmic arbitrageurs, and institutional liquidity pools ensures deep two-sided order books.

In NEPSE, order depth is thin. An aggressive buyer or seller entering market orders in a mid-cap hydropower, finance, or insurance counter can push the stock to its daily 10% upper or lower circuit limit within minutes. For value investors, this illiquidity provides substantial pricing dislocations (undervaluation during panic and overvaluation during euphoria), but it also introduces **exit risk**: entering a large illiquid position is far easier than exiting it during a bear market downturn.

### Depth: The Lack of Short Selling and Derivatives

The absence of short selling and exchange-traded derivatives on NEPSE fundamentally alters price discovery:

1. **One-Way Pressure Mechanism:** In mature exchanges, overvalued stocks face downward corrective pressure from short sellers borrowing shares to bet against inflated fundamentals. On NEPSE, an investor who knows a stock is drastically overvalued can only choose not to buy or sell what they already own. They cannot actively profit from the correction, allowing speculative bubbles to persist longer than in developed markets.
2. **No Direct Portfolio Hedging:** Without index futures or put options, fund managers and individual investors cannot purchase portfolio insurance against market-wide declines. Risk management on NEPSE relies almost entirely on asset allocation, cash management, and direct share liquidation.

## Lesson 7.4 — Who Owns NEPSE: Government Structure, Shareholding, and the Implications of State Ownership

### The Ownership Structure

Nepal Stock Exchange Ltd. is a government-controlled public enterprise. Its paid-up capital stands at **NPR 1.00 billion (NPR 100 Crore)**. 

```
                                  NEPSE SHAREHOLDING STRUCTURE
                 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                 │  Government of Nepal (Ministry of Finance)        58.66%   │
                 │  Rastriya Banijya Bank (State-owned BFI)          11.23%   │
                 │  Employees Provident Fund (Statutory Fund)        10.00%   │
                 │  Nepal Rastra Bank (Central Bank)                  9.50%   │
                 │  Other BFIs & Licensed Stockbrokers               10.60%   │
                 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Shareholder | Approximate Stake | Entity Classification |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Government of Nepal (Ministry of Finance)** | **58.66%** | Sovereign / State Treasury |
| **Rastriya Banijya Bank (RBB)** | **11.23%** | 100% State-owned Commercial Bank |
| **Employees Provident Fund (Karmachari Sanchaya Kosh - EPF)** | **10.00%** | Statutory Retirement Fund |
| **Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB)** | **9.50%** | Central Bank of Nepal |
| **Other Commercial Banks, Financial Institutions & Stockbrokers** | **10.60%** | Mixed Private & Semi-Public Intermediaries |

*Note: Originally, the Government of Nepal (58.66%) and Nepal Rastra Bank (34.60%) held over 93% combined equity. In line with financial sector reform recommendations, NRB divested 69.77% of its shareholding in 2019/2020 (primarily offloaded to Rastriya Banijya Bank), reducing its holding to 9.50%.*

### Structural Governance Tensions

The concentration of state ownership across multiple tiers creates several distinct institutional dynamics:

1. **State as Regulator, Exchange Owner, and Issuer:** The Government of Nepal owns 58.66% of the exchange, exercises administrative oversight via SEBON, and simultaneously retains substantial ownership stakes in major listed entities (e.g., Nepal Telecom, Agricultural Development Bank, Nepal Reinsurance Company, Hydroelectricity Investment and Development Company).
2. **The Central Bank Shareholder Dilemma:** Nepal Rastra Bank regulates the commercial banks that constitute the core of NEPSE's market capitalization, dictates margin lending terms, and sets interest rate policies that directly influence trading activity. Although NRB's mandate is macroeconomic stability, holding a 9.50% equity stake in the exchange it regulates creates an inherent governance overlap.
3. **Broker Equity Alignment:** Licensed stockbrokers and financial institutions collectively hold roughly 10.60% of the exchange. Because broker revenue derives primarily from transaction turnover commissions, the operational incentive structure across the exchange ecosystem naturally benefits from high-velocity trading rather than passive buy-and-hold investing.

## Lesson 7.5 — Market Capitalisation of NEPSE: What It Represents and What It Conceals

### Defining Market Capitalisation

Total Market Capitalisation represents the aggregate valuation that the market assigns to all outstanding shares of all listed companies:

$$\text{Market Capitalisation} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \text{Total Outstanding Shares}_i \times \text{Current Market Price}_i \right)$$

Between 2024 and 2026, NEPSE's aggregate market capitalisation has fluctuated between **NPR 3.5 trillion and NPR 4.8 trillion** (approximately **USD 25 to 36 billion**), depending on liquidity conditions.

### What Headline Market Cap Conceals

Using headline market capitalisation as a pure proxy for market size or applying the global "Buffett Indicator" (Total Market Cap to GDP Ratio) mechanically in Nepal can lead to flawed conclusions due to three structural factors:

```
                                  MARKET CAP DISTORTIONS
   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
   │   Free Float Gap      │   │ Sector Concentration  │   │  Corporate Exclusion  │
   │ Large promoter/state  │   │ Banks & hydro dominate│   │ Most real GDP happens │
   │ locked shares inflate │   │ headline value; non-  │   │ in unlisted private & │
   │ headline market cap.  │   │ financials under-rep. │   │ informal sectors.     │
   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘
```

#### 1. The Free Float Disconnect
Total market capitalization counts all issued shares, including state-held holdings and promoter shares subject to mandatory multi-year lock-in restrictions (e.g., the 3-year statutory lock-in period for hydropower promoters and institutional pre-IPO investors). In many state-linked and institutional counters, only 30% to 50% of the issued shares represent freely tradeable **public float**. Consequently, the liquid tradeable capitalization is significantly smaller than the headline figure.

#### 2. Sectoral Over-Concentration
The aggregate market cap is heavily weighted toward banking, financial institutions, insurance, and hydropower. If monetary policy easing causes financial stocks to surge, NEPSE's market cap will expand rapidly even if Nepal's broader non-financial corporate sector is struggling with economic headwinds.

#### 3. The National Economy Inclusion Gap
NEPSE lists approximately 250+ equity companies. However, the vast majority of Nepal's real economic output — agriculture, wholesale and retail trade, real estate, transportation, construction, hospitality, and private remittance-backed enterprises — operates through unlisted private companies, sole proprietorships, and informal entities. A high Market Cap-to-GDP ratio on NEPSE indicates financial asset liquidity rather than a comprehensive valuation of the national economy.

## Lesson 7.6 — The NEPSE Index: Construction, Sector Weightage, and Why It Can Mislead

### Index Construction Methodology

The benchmark **NEPSE Index** is a **market capitalisation-weighted index**. It measures the aggregate value change of all listed equities relative to the base period:

$$\text{NEPSE Index} = \frac{\text{Current Aggregate Market Capitalisation of All Listed Scrips}}{\text{Base Period Aggregate Market Capitalisation (12 Feb 1994)}} \times 100$$

Alongside the headline index, NEPSE computes:
- **Sensitive Index:** Measures the performance of Class 'A' companies meeting specific capital, profitability, dividend, and disclosure benchmarks.
- **Float Index:** Calculated strictly on the basis of tradeable **free-float shares**, excluding locked promoter and state holdings.
- **Sensitive Float Index:** Tracks the free-float performance of Class 'A' listed companies.

### Sector Composition and Index Weightings

The sectoral distribution of NEPSE has evolved. While commercial banks once accounted for over 60% of the index, rapid hydropower IPOs and insurance sector growth have broadened the base:

```
                            APPROXIMATE SECTOR BREAKDOWN (BY MARKET CAP)
        ┌────────────────────────────────┬──────────────┬────────────────────────┐
        │ Sector                         │ Weight (%)   │ Listed Entities (Est.) │
        ├────────────────────────────────┼──────────────┼────────────────────────┤
        │ Commercial Banks               │ ~28 – 35%    │ 20                     │
        │ Hydropower                     │ ~15 – 18%    │ 90 – 100+              │
        │ Life & Non-Life Insurance      │ ~11 – 15%    │ ~28 – 30               │
        │ Microfinance                   │ ~6 – 8%      │ ~55 – 60               │
        │ Development Banks              │ ~5 – 6%      │ ~17                    │
```

*Weights fluctuate continuously with daily price movements, new IPO listings, right share capitalizations, and market cycles.*

### Why the Headline Index Can Be Deceptive

Because the index is market-cap weighted, a few large-cap banking, telecom, or reinsurance stocks can distort the perceived direction of the market:

1. **Masked Sectoral Rotations:** A sharp rally in small-cap hydropower and finance stocks can occur while the headline NEPSE Index stays flat or declines if large commercial banks are experiencing selling pressure.
2. **Benchmark Mismatch:** Evaluating a non-financial or hydropower-heavy portfolio against the headline NEPSE Index is methodologically misleading. Disciplined investors measure their holdings against the corresponding **sectoral sub-indices**.

## Lesson 7.7 — Sub-Indices: Banking, Hydropower, Microfinance, Insurance, Manufacturing — What Each Tracks

### 1. The Banking Sub-Index (`BANKINGIND`)
Tracks Nepal's 20 commercial banks (Class 'A' BFIs). 
- **Key Fundamental Drivers:** NRB Monetary Policy (repo rate, CRR of 4.0%, SLR of 12.0%, CD ratio statutory ceiling of 90%), Net Interest Margin (NIM), gross Non-Performing Assets (NPA/NPL), loan-loss provisioning requirements, and counter-cyclical capital buffers (CCB).
- **Behavioral Character:** Highly liquid, institutionally owned, dividend-focused, and directly responsive to central bank interest rate decisions.

### 2. The Hydropower Sub-Index (`HYDROPOWIND`)
Tracks over 90+ listed hydropower generation and development companies.
- **Key Fundamental Drivers:** River hydrology (seasonality of run-of-river output between wet monsoon months and dry winter months), Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) tariffs with Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA) (Take-or-Pay vs. Take-and-Pay terms), project debt servicing, transmission line evacuation capacity, and cross-border electricity export agreements with India and Bangladesh.
- **Structural Nuance:** Promoter and local-allotted shares are subject to a **3-year statutory lock-in period** from the date of commercial operation or public allotment. Lock-in expiry dates often release massive share supply into the secondary market, creating predictable price volatility.

### 3. The Microfinance Sub-Index (`MICROFININD`)
Tracks over 55+ microfinance financial institutions (Class 'D' BFIs).
- **Key Fundamental Drivers:** Rural household income, agriculture cycles, NRB regulatory caps on lending interest rates (statutory ceiling of 15%), restrictions on wholesale borrowing spreads, and asset quality in uncollateralized group-lending loan books.
- **Behavioral Character:** High historical volatility, substantial retail speculative participation, and high sensitivity to regulatory interventions and rural credit distress.

### 4. Life and Non-Life Insurance Sub-Indices (`LIFEINSUIND` & `NONLIFEIND`)
Regulated by the **Nepal Insurance Authority (Nepal Bima Pradhikaran - नेपाल बीमा प्राधिकरण)** under the Insurance Act, 2079 (2022).
- **Key Fundamental Drivers:** Minimum paid-up capital requirements (mandated at NPR 5 Arba for Life and NPR 2.5 Arba for Non-Life, driving extensive industry mergers), premium growth, life fund investment yields, claims ratios (especially catastrophic flood and earthquake claims for non-life), and reinsurance treaties with state-backed and international reinsurers.

### 5. Manufacturing & Processing (`MANUFACTUREIND`), Hotels & Tourism (`HOTELIND`), and Others
- **Manufacturing:** Encompasses cement, distillery, and consumer goods manufacturers (e.g., Shivam Cements, Himalayan Distillery). Characterized by high operating leverage, fuel/energy tariff sensitivity, and thin public float in several counters.
- **Hotels & Tourism:** Tracks listed luxury hotels and cable car operators. Driven by foreign tourist arrivals, domestic leisure travel, and banquet/conference revenue.
- **Others & Investment:** Contains high-capitalization state and institutional giants like Nepal Telecom (NTC), Nepal Reinsurance Company (NRIC), Citizen Investment Trust (CIT), and Hydroelectricity Investment and Development Company (HIDCL).

## Lesson 7.8 — Bull and Bear Phases in NEPSE History: The 2016 Peak, 2021 Boom, and Subsequent Cycles

### The Structural Anatomy of a NEPSE Cycle

NEPSE market cycles are predominantly **liquidity- and policy-driven**, rather than classical corporate earnings-growth cycles. The transmission mechanism follows a repeatable institutional pattern:

```
                                  THE NEPSE CYCLE TRANSMISSION
   ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐
   │  NRB Monetary Policy  │ ────> │ Banking System Liquidity│ ────> │ Equity Market Velocity │
   │ Rate cuts, relaxed CD │       │ Margin loan expansion,│       │ Retail momentum surges│
   │ ratio & risk weights  │       │ plunging deposit rates│       │ until policy tightens │
   └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘
```

1. **Easing Phase:** NRB cuts the policy repo rate, lowers deposit interest rates, and eases margin lending guidelines. Banks experience excess loanable funds.
2. **Margin Expansion & Retail Onboarding:** Low deposit rates push household savings out of fixed deposits into the secondary stock market. Broker margin loans expand rapidly.
3. **Momentum & Narrative Climax:** Social media groups, news portals, and retail networks amplify speculative narratives. Fundamentals detach from market prices as high-beta stocks (microfinance, small hydro, finance) trade at extreme multiples.
4. **Regulatory Tightening:** Concerned by inflation, high import bills, or asset price inflation, NRB raises interest rates, tightens bank lending criteria, or lowers margin loan thresholds.
5. **Deleveraging & Forced Liquidation:** Higher interest rates pull capital back to bank deposits. Margin calls trigger forced selling through broker accounts, initiating an accelerating downward spiral until excess leverage is flushed from the system.

### Case Studies of Major Cycles

#### 1. The 2014–2016 Bull Market (Peak: 1,881.45 — July 2016)
- **Primary Driver:** Massive liquidity injection following post-2015 earthquake relief spending, combined with NRB's directive requiring commercial banks to quadruple paid-up capital from Rs 2 Arba to Rs 8 Arba. Investors aggressively bid up bank stocks anticipating massive right share and bonus share distributions.
- **The Turn:** Once capital requirements were met and NRB tightened loanable funds ratios, liquidity dried up. NEPSE entered a 32-month bear market, declining ~41.5% to bottom at 1,100.58 in March 2019.

#### 2. The 2020–2021 Pandemic Bull Market (Peak: 3,198.60 — August 2021)
- **Primary Driver:** During COVID-19 lockdowns, commercial lending halted while remittance inflows surged. NRB slashed policy rates to record lows. With real interest rates negative and physical businesses closed, liquidity flooded NEPSE via online TMS/MeroShare platforms.
- **The Turn:** In its 2021/22 Monetary Policy, NRB introduced the strict **4/12 Crore margin loan cap** (capping share-backed borrowing at Rs 4 crore per institution and Rs 12 crore aggregate), raised the risk weight on share loans to 150%, and hiked policy rates. NEPSE plunged ~43% over 13 months, bottoming at **1,815.14 in September 2022**.

#### 3. The 2024–2026 Liquidity Rebound
- **Primary Driver:** Beginning in FY 2023/24 and FY 2024/25, NRB dismantled restrictive share-lending constraints: reducing the risk weight of margin loans back to 125%, abolishing the Rs 12-crore institutional cap, and continuously reducing the policy repo rate amid record-high banking system liquidity.
- **The Result:** NEPSE rebounded forcefully back toward the ~3,000 zone in mid-2024, setting an all-time single-day trading turnover record of **NPR 29.95 billion (Rs 2,995 crore) on August 15, 2024**.

### The NEPSE Investor's Cycle Checklist

Before allocating capital in NEPSE, evaluate where the market stands on the macro cycle checklist:

- [ ] **NRB Monetary Policy Stance:** Is the central bank easing (lowering repo rates / expanding credit) or tightening (hiking rates / restricting credit)?
- [ ] **Interbank Liquidity & CD Ratio:** Are commercial banks holding excess liquidity, or is the system-wide CD ratio near the 90% statutory ceiling?
- [ ] **Margin Lending Risk Weights & Limits:** What are the current regulatory risk weights and aggregate loan limits on share-collateralized lending?
- [ ] **Hydropower Lock-In Calendar:** Are significant promoter share tranches in your target hydro companies reaching the end of their 3-year lock-in period?
- [ ] **Valuation vs Historical Median:** How are the Banking, Insurance, and Hydro sub-indices priced in terms of P/E and P/BV relative to their 5-year cyclical medians?
- [ ] **Social Media Euphoria vs Disillusionment:** Are unvetted retail tip channels dominating the narrative (late-stage euphoria), or is market sentiment characterized by pervasive apathy (trough accumulation)?

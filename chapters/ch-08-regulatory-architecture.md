> *Markets do not regulate themselves. The history of financial markets worldwide is a history of what happens when they try.*

In Nepal, the regulatory architecture governing the capital market is uniquely complex. Multiple institutions exercise overlapping authority over different aspects of market operations, and the boundaries between their respective mandates are tightly intertwined with macroeconomic and sectoral policies. Understanding who regulates what, how those regulators are structured, what powers they wield, and — critically — what political and institutional incentives shape how they exercise those powers, is not an academic exercise for the NEPSE investor. It is foundational market intelligence. 

Regulatory decisions in Nepal have repeatedly moved the market by 10–20% within single sessions. Regulatory bottlenecks have prolonged market stress, while timely regulatory easing has historically catalyzed major bull runs. This chapter maps the regulatory landscape completely: SEBON, NRB, CDSC, NEPSE as exchange operator, merchant bankers, disclosure rules, investor protection mechanisms, the governing legislative acts, and the underlying political economy.

## Lesson 8.1 — SEBON: Mandate, Powers, Structural Limitations, and Political Economy

### What SEBON Is

The **Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON)** — *Dhitopatra Board Nepal* (धितोपत्र बोर्ड नेपाल) — is the apex regulatory authority for Nepal's capital market, established on June 7, 1993, under the Securities Exchange Act 2040 and reconstituted under the comprehensive **Securities Act, 2063 (2006 AD)**. It is an autonomous statutory body under the Ministry of Finance, mandated to regulate and develop the securities market, safeguard investor interests, and uphold market integrity.

SEBON's regulatory remit covers:
- **Market Infrastructure:** Nepal Stock Exchange (NEPSE) and CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC).
- **Intermediaries:** Licensed stockbrokerage firms, merchant bankers (issue managers, underwriters, portfolio managers), depository participants (DPs), and credit rating agencies (e.g., ICRA Nepal, CARE Ratings Nepal, Infomerics Nepal).
- **Collective Investment Schemes:** Mutual fund schemes, specialized investment funds (private equity/venture capital), and fund managers.
- **Listed Entities:** Public issuance approvals (IPOs, FPOs, Rights, Debentures) and corporate disclosure compliance.

SEBON's governing Board is chaired by the **Chairman** (appointed by the Government of Nepal on the recommendation of a selection committee) and includes senior representatives from the Ministry of Finance, Ministry of Law, Nepal Rastra Bank, the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nepal (ICAN), the Federation of Nepalese Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FNCCI), and independent market experts.

### SEBON's Statutory Powers

Under the Securities Act 2063, SEBON wields broad legal authority:
1. **Licensing & Registration:** Power to grant, renew, suspend, or cancel licenses of stockbrokers, merchant bankers, market operators, and credit rating agencies.
2. **Issue Approvals:** Authority to approve or reject prospectuses for public offerings (IPOs, Rights, Debentures, Mutual Funds).
3. **Surveillance & Investigation:** Power to summon records, inspect books of accounts, conduct investigative inquiries, and interrogate individuals suspected of market manipulation or insider trading.
4. **Enforcement & Penalties:** Authority to levy financial penalties, freeze suspect trading accounts, issue binding directives, and file formal criminal charges through the Office of the Attorney General.

### SEBON's Structural Limitations

Despite its extensive statutory mandate, SEBON faces several institutional constraints:
- **Technical & Forensic Capacity:** SEBON operates with a relatively compact technical staff. Advanced forensic data analysis, algorithmic surveillance, and deep investigative auditing of complex syndicate schemes require continuous capacity building.
- **Enforcement Deterrence Gap:** Historically, regulatory fines for market misconduct have often been modest compared to the abnormal speculative profits gained from price manipulation. Criminal prosecutions have been rare, weakening the deterrence effect against insider trading.
- **Legislative Dependencies:** Comprehensive structural changes (e.g., launching formal equity derivatives, short selling, or green bond frameworks) require parliamentary amendments or cabinet-level approvals, which are subject to legislative delays.

## Lesson 8.2 — NRB: How a Central Bank Becomes the Single Most Powerful Force in NEPSE

### NRB's De Facto Supremacy Over NEPSE

Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) is the central bank of Nepal, established under the **Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058 (2002 AD)**. Its statutory mandate focuses on macroeconomic price stability, foreign exchange reserve management, payment system security, and prudential banking supervision. NRB does not formally regulate the stock exchange or non-bank public companies.

Yet, in practice, **NRB is the single most powerful driver of NEPSE's valuation and volatility cycles.** This occurs because commercial banks, development banks, finance companies, and microfinance institutions comprise the core of NEPSE's capitalization, and bank credit provides the primary liquidity fuel for equity transactions.

### Key Transmission Mechanisms

```
                               NRB POLICY TRANSMISSION TO NEPSE
   ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐
   │  NRB Monetary Policy  │ ────> │ Banking System Credit │ ────> │ Equity Market Prices  │
   │  Repo rate, CD ratio, │       │ Loanable liquidity &  │       │ Asset valuations and  │
   │   risk weights & caps │       │ margin finance volume │       │ trading volumes swing │
   └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘
```

#### 1. The CD Ratio (Credit-to-Deposit Ratio)
NRB mandates that commercial banks maintain an aggregate Credit-to-Deposit (CD) ratio below **90%**. When the banking system's CD ratio approaches 90%, banks immediately restrict incremental lending across all sectors, including share-collateralized margin loans. Conversely, when the CD ratio drops into the 78–82% range, banks experience substantial loanable liquidity, reducing borrowing rates and fueling equity investment.

#### 2. Margin Lending Regulations (Share-Collateralized Loans)
NRB directly regulates margin lending terms:
- **Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio:** Typically set at a maximum of **70%** of the 180-day (or 120-day) average share price or current market price, whichever is lower.
- **Risk Weighting on Share Loans:** Adjusting risk weights between **100%, 125%, and 150%** directly influences how much regulatory capital banks must hold against share loans.
- **Borrowing Ceilings:** Historical directives such as the 4/12-crore ceiling directly compressed institutional borrowing in 2021/2022; subsequent relaxation in 2024 provided strong liquidity relief.

#### 3. Interest Rate Corridor & Fixed Deposit Competition
In a retail-dominated market, bank Fixed Deposit (FD) rates serve as the primary hurdle rate for equity investors. When NRB cuts policy rates and banking liquidity pushes FD rates down to 5–6%, capital flows out of deposits into the stock market in search of higher yields. When FD rates rise to 10–12%, retail capital exits equities for guaranteed cash returns.

| NRB Policy Instrument | Tightening Effect on NEPSE | Easing Effect on NEPSE |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Policy Repo Rate** | Raises cost of leverage; FDs become attractive. | Lowers cost of leverage; equities gain yield appeal. |
| **CD Ratio Ceiling (90%)** | Forces credit curbs; triggers margin deleveraging. | Expands credit headroom; spurs loanable funds. |
| **Risk Weight on Share Loans** | Increases bank capital cost; curbs margin lending. | Reduces capital requirement; expands share lending. |
| **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR 4%)** | Freezes bank reserves; reduces loanable supply. | Releases liquidity into the banking system. |
| **Standing Liquidity Facility (SLF)** | Tightens interbank cash rates. | Injects short-term liquidity into commercial banks. |

## Lesson 8.3 — CDSC: Clearing, Settlement, and Depository Functions

### Depository, Clearing, and Settlement Architecture

**CDS and Clearing Limited (CDSC)** was established in 2010 as a wholly-owned subsidiary of NEPSE to provide modern electronic book-entry depository, multilateral trade clearing, and electronic settlement services.

```
                                 THE CDSC TRANSACTION PIPELINE
   ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐
   │ 1. Trading Execution  │ ────> │  2. Multilateral Net  │ ────> │ 3. Final Settlement   │
   │ Buyer & Seller orders │       │ CDSC nets securities  │       │ Securities to Demat;  │
   │ matched on NEPSE NATS │       │ obligations & cash    │       │ Cash via Clearing Bank│
   └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘
```

1. **The Depository Function (DEMAT Accounts):** CDSC maintains the ultimate electronic ledger of all beneficial ownership across listed shares, debentures, and mutual funds. Every investor holds an electronic DEMAT account through registered Depository Participants (DPs).
2. **The Clearing Function (Multilateral Netting):** At the close of each trading session, CDSC processes all trade data executed on NEPSE. Instead of settling every individual trade gross, CDSC nets out the buy and sell obligations for each broker, minimizing cash and scrip transfers.
3. **The Settlement Function (T+2 Cycle):** Nepal operates on a standard **T+2 settlement cycle** (settlement completed two business days after trade execution). Shares are transferred to the buyer's Demat account and funds are routed to the seller's broker account via designated clearing banks (including Global IME Bank as lead clearing bank, NIMB, Prabhu, Siddhartha) integrated with NCHL payment systems.
4. **MeroShare:** CDSC's centralized retail portal enables investors to manage Demat portfolios, execute electronic Delivery Instruction Slips (EDIS) for share transfers, track corporate actions, and apply for IPOs/Rights issues under the C-ASBA system.

## Lesson 8.4 — NEPSE as Exchange Operator: Listing Rules, Trading Rules, and Delisting Powers

### NEPSE's Operational Mandate

The **Nepal Stock Exchange Limited (NEPSE)** operates the marketplace where trading occurs. While SEBON regulates the broader market, NEPSE enforces day-to-day trading operations, listing agreements, order routing through NATS, and volatility mechanisms.

### Circuit Breakers and Price Band Mechanism

To curb extreme intraday volatility and prevent panic spirals, NEPSE enforces a standardized, multi-tiered circuit breaker framework:

```
                            NEPSE MARKET-WIDE CIRCUIT BREAKERS
   ┌──────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┐
   │ Trigger Threshold                │ Trading Time Window  │ Market Halt Duration      │
   ├──────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
   │ 4% Index Movement (Up/Down)      │ 1st Hour (to 12:00PM)│ 20 Minutes Suspension     │
   │ 5% Index Movement (Up/Down)      │ 2nd Hour (to 1:00 PM)│ 40 Minutes Suspension     │
   │ 6% Index Movement (Up/Down)      │ At any trading time  │ Closed for Rest of Day    │
   └──────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┘
```

- **Individual Stock Price Limits:** Listed equities are subject to a **±10% maximum daily price band** from the previous day's closing price.
- **Pre-Open Price Band:** Orders entered during the 10:30 AM – 10:45 AM pre-open session must remain within **±5%** of the previous day's close.

## Lesson 8.5 — Merchant Bankers and Issue Managers: Their Role in IPOs and Rights Issues

Merchant bankers (licensed by SEBON) act as the critical issue managers, underwriters, and advisors for capital formation:
- **Prospectus Due Diligence:** Drafting and verifying prospectus disclosures regarding financial health, promoter backgrounds, and project feasibility.
- **Public Subscription Management:** Managing the Centralized Applications Supported by Blocked Amount (C-ASBA) system in coordination with commercial banks and CDSC.
- **Underwriting Commitments:** Committing institutional capital to absorb unsubscribed public shares when an issue is underwritten.

## Lesson 8.6 — SEBON's Disclosure Requirements: What Listed Companies Must Publish and When

Transparency is the foundation of investor protection. SEBON mandates four primary categories of continuous disclosure:

| Disclosure Category | Mandatory Frequency | Statutory Deadline | Key Analytical Information |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Quarterly Financials** | Quarterly (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4) | Within **30 days** of quarter end | Balance sheet, P&L, EPS, Book Value, NPL/NPA ratios. |
| **Audited Annual Report** | Annual | Within **6 months** of FY end (15 July) | Comprehensive NFRS audited financials, auditor notes, related party deals. |
| **Material Events (Price Sensitive)** | Event-driven | **Immediately (Same Day)** | Dividend declarations, bonus/rights proposals, mergers, PPA deals. |
| **Shareholding & Pledges** | Quarterly | Within **15 days** of quarter end | Promoter holding changes, promoter share lock-in status, share pledges. |
| **AGM Notice** | Annual | Minimum **21 days** prior to AGM | Agenda, proposed dividend approvals, director election details. |

## Lesson 8.7 — Investor Protection Fund and Grievance Redressal in Nepal

### Investor Protection Fund (IPF)
Established under the Securities Act 2063, the **Investor Protection Fund** is maintained by SEBON using broker contributions and regulatory penalties. It serves as a statutory compensation fund for retail investors specifically in cases of **broker insolvency or fraudulent non-settlement** of client funds/shares (it does not insure against normal market losses).

### Investor Grievance Mechanism
Investors can register formal complaints regarding delayed bonus share credits, unauthorized broker transactions, or corporate disclosure failures via SEBON's **Online Grievance Redressal Portal** or directly with NEPSE's Market Supervision Department.

## Lesson 8.8 — Key Acts Governing the Market: Securities Act 2063, Company Act 2063, BAFIA

Nepal's capital market operates under an integrated tripartite legislative framework:

```
                                KEY GOVERNING LEGISLATION
   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
   │  Securities Act 2063  │   │   Company Act 2063    │   │      BAFIA 2073       │
   │ Regulates SEBON, NEPSE│   │ Corporate governance, │   │ Bank licensing, NPLs, │
   │ brokers, IPOs & fraud │   │ shareholder rights,   │   │ capital adequacy &    │
   │ prohibitions.         │   │ AGMs, and mergers.    │   │ dividend approvals.   │
   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘
```

| Primary Legislation | Year Enacted | Core Jurisdiction & Key Provisions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Securities Act, 2063** | 2006 AD | Establishes SEBON; regulates public offerings, broker licensing, insider trading prohibitions, and the Investor Protection Fund. |
| **Companies Act, 2063** | 2006 AD | Governs corporate incorporation, AGM voting rights, rights issues, bonus share issuances, and corporate liquidation priorities. |
| **Banks & Financial Institutions Act (BAFIA), 2073** | 2017 AD | Governs licensing of Class 'A', 'B', 'C', and 'D' BFIs; regulates capital adequacy, promoter lock-in, and statutory dividend clearance by NRB. |
| **Insurance Act, 2079** | 2022 AD | Replaces Beema Samiti with the **Nepal Insurance Authority**; mandates capital requirements (Rs 5 Arba for Life, Rs 2.5 Arba for Non-Life). |
| **Securities Registration & Issuance Regulation, 2073** | 2016 AD | Dictates prospectus filing standards, IPO/FPO pricing methodologies, and C-ASBA allotment procedures. |

## Lesson 8.9 — The Political Economy of Regulation: How Incentives Shape NRB, SEBON, and NEA Decisions

Understanding the formal laws provides the structural framework, but understanding the **political economy** explains how regulatory bodies actually behave in real market conditions:

### 1. SEBON's Institutional Pressures
- **IPO Approval Bias:** Approving public offerings creates diffuse public participation and satisfies corporate promoters. Rejecting low-quality issues generates direct pushback from influential sponsors.
- **Enforcement Asymmetries:** Diffuse retail investors rarely organize to lobby for proactive enforcement, while organized broker syndicates and promoter groups possess concentrated lobbying power.

### 2. NRB's Macroprudential Dilemma
- Monetary tightening cycles are politically unpopular because they depress stock prices and raise loan EMIs for retail borrowers. Consequently, NRB tightening interventions are often delayed until macroeconomic imbalances (such as foreign exchange reserve depletion or severe inflation) make credit contraction unavoidable.

### 3. Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA): Hydropower's De Facto Regulator
- For over 90+ listed hydropower companies, **NEA is the primary operational gatekeeper.**
- NEA's Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) determine long-term revenue predictability. Delays in constructing national transmission lines or substations create severe evacuation bottlenecks, impacting listed developer earnings.
- Progress on cross-border electricity trading agreements with India and Bangladesh directly determines NEA's off-take capacity and foreign exchange revenue potential.

### Political Economy Checklist for NEPSE Investors

- [ ] **Leadership Shifts:** Has there been a recent change in SEBON Chairman or NRB Governor? Anticipate shifts in regulatory stance.
- [ ] **Monetary Policy Direction:** Are policy rates, CRR, and margin loan risk weights in an easing or tightening phase?
- [ ] **Systemic CD Ratio:** Is banking sector loanable liquidity ample (CD < 80%) or constrained (CD > 88%)?
- [ ] **Infrastructure & Transmission Timelines:** For hydropower holdings, are dedicated evacuation substations operational?
- [ ] **Promoter Lock-In Dates:** Are target companies approaching their 3-year promoter lock-in expiration?

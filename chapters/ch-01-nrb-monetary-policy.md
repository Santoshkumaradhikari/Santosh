# Part I · Chapter 1: Nepal Rastra Bank’s Monetary Policy Framework

**First published 31 Aug 2026 · Last verified 31 Aug 2026**

> **Scope note:** This chapter explains how Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) makes and transmits monetary policy. It is an educational framework, not a forecast, a trading signal or investment advice. Rates, rules and data change; always verify the current NRB release before acting.

## The one-sentence answer

Nepal Rastra Bank uses interest rates, liquidity operations, reserve requirements, foreign-exchange management and selected macroprudential tools to pursue price and balance-of-payments stability while supporting sustainable growth. The effect on NEPSE is indirect, variable and delayed.

That sentence is deliberately precise. NRB does not set share prices, commercial-bank loan demand or company profits. It changes the conditions in which those outcomes are produced.

## 1.1 What monetary policy is—and what it is not

Monetary policy is the central bank’s use of its balance sheet, operating instruments, policy rates and regulatory authority to influence:

- the price and availability of short-term money;
- the incentives to save, borrow and lend;
- the quantity and composition of liquidity in the banking system;
- inflation and inflation expectations;
- the external balance and foreign-exchange reserves;
- financial-system stability.

It is not the same as fiscal policy. The Government of Nepal controls taxation, expenditure, borrowing and the budget. It is not the same as prudential supervision. NRB supervises banks and financial institutions, but a supervisory action is not necessarily a monetary-policy action. It is not the same as a stock-market policy. A change in the policy rate may influence NEPSE through several channels, but it does not establish a fair value for any listed company.

## 1.2 NRB’s mandate and Nepal’s constraints

The NRB Act gives the central bank a mandate centred on price stability and balance-of-payments stability, alongside monetary and financial-system management. Nepal’s framework must also operate within several structural constraints:

1. **The Indian-rupee peg.** The Nepali rupee’s fixed relationship with the Indian rupee is an important nominal anchor. It supports trade and financial predictability, but it limits the freedom to run an entirely independent exchange-rate policy.
2. **High import dependence.** Domestic demand can quickly become an import demand and foreign-exchange demand. Excess monetary expansion may therefore appear in the external sector before it appears only in domestic inflation.
3. **Remittance dependence.** Remittance inflows support household income, deposits and foreign-exchange reserves, but they are exposed to destination-country employment, exchange rates and migration policy.
4. **A bank-dominated financial system.** Bank balance sheets are the principal route through which many monetary-policy changes reach businesses and households.
5. **Shallow market depth.** In a less diversified financial market, liquidity and expectations can move asset prices quickly, even when the underlying policy change is modest.

These constraints explain why Nepal cannot be analysed by copying a large, financially deep economy’s playbook.

## 1.3 The operating target: the weighted-average interbank rate

A central bank needs an operating target: a market variable it can influence at high frequency. NRB uses the weighted-average interbank rate (WAIBR) as a key operating target. The WAIBR is the rate at which eligible financial institutions lend to one another in the short-term interbank market, weighted by transaction value.

The operating logic is:

```text
NRB announces a policy-rate corridor and stance
              ↓
NRB injects or absorbs liquidity through operations
              ↓
Interbank rate moves toward the desired operating level
              ↓
Short-term market rates and expectations adjust
              ↓
Deposit, lending, bond and asset-pricing conditions respond
```

The WAIBR is not the same as the average lending rate paid by a household or company. It is a wholesale, short-term market rate. Its usefulness is greatest when combined with its position inside the interest-rate corridor and with evidence about deposits, credit demand and bank pricing.

NRB’s July 2026 macroeconomic report described a system with ample liquidity, low short-term rates and subdued credit growth, while noting that remittances were supporting liquidity but credit demand and absorption remained weaker. [NRB Macroeconomic Report, July 2026](https://www.nrb.org.np/ofg/macroeconomic-report-july-2026/)

## 1.4 The interest-rate corridor

The interest-rate corridor gives the short-term market a floor and a ceiling around the operating target.

### Lower bound: Standing Deposit Facility

The Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) allows eligible institutions to place surplus funds with NRB at a specified rate, subject to the applicable rules. It helps establish a floor under short-term rates because an institution with surplus liquidity has an alternative to lending below the facility’s return.

### Centre: policy or repo rate

The policy rate is the key signal of the monetary-policy stance. It influences the cost at which eligible institutions can obtain short-term liquidity through repo-related operations. A lower rate generally signals easier monetary conditions, but the actual effect depends on whether banks and borrowers respond.

### Upper bound: Standing Liquidity Facility and bank rate

The Standing Liquidity Facility (SLF) provides a higher-cost source of last-resort or standing liquidity subject to eligibility and collateral rules. The bank rate is also an important upper-bound or lender-of-last-resort reference in the framework. The exact operational design and published rates must be checked in the latest policy and implementation documents.

### Why the corridor matters

| Corridor element | Economic function | Investor interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| SDF / floor | Gives surplus liquidity a low-risk placement option | Persistent WAIBR near the floor suggests abundant liquidity, not necessarily strong credit demand |
| Policy rate | Main signal of intended short-term stance | A cut may support rates and valuation, but may also signal weak growth |
| SLF / upper bound | Limits extreme short-term funding costs | A WAIBR approaching the ceiling can indicate liquidity stress |
| WAIBR | Operational market outcome | Observe persistence, volume and distance from the corridor |

A policy-rate cut is not automatically bullish. It can mean easier funding conditions, or it can be a response to weak activity, falling demand or financial stress.

## 1.5 NRB’s main instruments

### 1. Open-market operations

NRB injects liquidity through repo or related purchases and absorbs liquidity through reverse-repo, deposit-collection or sale operations, according to the applicable procedure. These transactions change the amount and price of short-term funds.

- **Liquidity injection:** increases short-term funds available to the system, all else equal.
- **Liquidity absorption:** removes or sterilises excess funds, all else equal.
- **Fine-tuning:** responds to temporary deviations in the interbank market.
- **Structural operations:** address more persistent liquidity conditions.

The direction alone is not enough. Record the amount offered, amount accepted, tenor, cut-off rate and whether the operation was fully subscribed.

### 2. Standing facilities

Standing facilities give institutions a predictable place to deposit surplus funds or obtain eligible liquidity. They support the corridor and reduce extreme overnight-rate volatility, but they do not force banks to lend to private borrowers.

### 3. Cash Reserve Ratio

The Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) requires banks or BFIs to hold a specified portion of certain deposit liabilities as cash reserve according to NRB rules. A CRR change can alter the liquidity immediately available to an institution.

CRR is not a complete measure of lending capacity. Capital adequacy, liquidity, CD-ratio rules, loan demand, underwriting and risk appetite may bind before CRR does.

### 4. Statutory Liquidity Ratio

The Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) requires eligible institutions to maintain specified liquid assets. SLR supports resilience and liquidity management, but it should not be treated as a simple “money not available for lending” percentage. Eligible assets may include instruments that remain part of the financial system and may generate income.

The applicable SLR differs by institution type and can change. Never use a historical SLR in a current calculation without citing the relevant NRB rule.

### 5. Credit and macroprudential tools

NRB can use tools that target financial stability or credit allocation, including:

- CD-ratio requirements;
- capital and risk-weight adjustments;
- loan-to-value or collateral rules;
- sectoral lending guidance;
- large-exposure and single-obligor limits;
- margin-lending requirements;
- restructuring, classification and provisioning rules.

These instruments are not identical to the policy rate. A rate cut can coexist with tighter sectoral rules, and a rate increase can coexist with liquidity support.

### 6. Foreign-exchange operations and the peg

Because Nepal operates with a fixed exchange relationship with the Indian rupee, foreign-exchange reserves and external-sector conditions are central to monetary management. NRB must consider:

- remittances;
- imports and exports;
- tourism receipts;
- foreign loans and grants;
- capital flows;
- reserve adequacy;
- India’s inflation and monetary conditions.

A monetary setting that is comfortable domestically may become difficult if it creates excessive import pressure or threatens external stability.

## 1.6 How to read a monetary-policy announcement

Do not read only the headline rate. Extract the following in order:

1. **Stance:** accommodative, neutral, cautious, restrictive or another official description.
2. **Objective:** inflation, reserves, growth, financial stability and external balance.
3. **Operating target:** WAIBR and its desired relationship with the corridor.
4. **Rate changes:** policy, SDF, SLF, bank rate and related facilities.
5. **Quantity changes:** CRR, SLR, CD ratio, refinancing, deposit collection and OMO rules.
6. **Sector measures:** real estate, share-backed lending, agriculture, SMEs, energy and other targeted areas.
7. **Forecasts:** money supply, private-sector credit, inflation, reserves and growth.
8. **Implementation language:** immediate, phased, review-based or subject to a circular.
9. **Risk language:** what could cause the policy to change.
10. **Reaction function:** which future data will matter to the next decision.

The policy statement is a plan and signal. The subsequent circulars, monthly data and actual operations reveal implementation.

## 1.7 Policy transmission to banks and the economy

The transmission chain is not one-way or instantaneous.

```text
Policy signal
   ↓
Interbank liquidity and short-term rates
   ↓
Deposit pricing, lending rates and bond yields
   ↓
Loan demand, approval and disbursement
   ↓
Consumption, investment and imports
   ↓
Corporate revenue, bank margins and asset quality
   ↓
Inflation, reserves, earnings and equity valuations
```

Transmission can fail or weaken at every step:

- Banks may have liquidity but weak loan demand.
- Borrowers may be unwilling to invest despite lower rates.
- Banks may protect margins rather than pass through the full cut.
- Fixed deposits may reprice more slowly than loans.
- NPLs may absorb the benefit through provisions.
- Lower rates may improve valuations but signal weak growth.
- Remittances may increase deposits without increasing domestic borrowing.

The right question is therefore not “Did NRB cut rates?” but “Which link in the chain changed, and what evidence confirms that it reached the next link?”

## 1.8 A current baseline: FY 2025/26

NRB’s annual report for FY 2025/26 should be treated as the baseline for this chapter’s current context. Its reported headline indicators include:

| Indicator | FY 2025/26 / mid-July 2026 | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Average CPI inflation | 3.08% | Low average inflation during the year |
| Mid-July CPI inflation | 5.14% | End-period pressure was higher than the annual average |
| Broad-money growth | 13.4% | Monetary expansion exceeded the previous year |
| Deposits at BFIs | NPR 8,276.93bn; +13.9% | Strong deposit mobilisation |
| Private-sector credit | NPR 5,857.06bn; +6.5% | Credit growth lagged deposit growth |
| WAIBR at end-period | 2.75% | Short-term rate close to the lower-liquidity environment |
| Commercial-bank average deposit rate | 3.21% | Lower funding cost than the prior year |
| Commercial-bank average lending rate | 6.55% | Lower borrowing rate than the prior year |
| Remittances | +37.1% in NPR terms; +28.1% in USD terms | Strong external inflow support |
| Gross FX reserves | NPR 3,897.67bn / USD 25.31bn | Reported reserve buffer was substantial |

Source: NRB, *Current Macroeconomic and Financial Situation Based on Annual Data of FY 2025/26*. Values are period-specific and should not be reused after a newer release without re-verification. [NRB annual FY 2025/26 report](https://www.nrb.org.np/contents/uploads/2026/08/Current-Macroeconomic-and-Financial-Situation-English-1.pdf)

### What this baseline teaches

The combination of strong deposits and slower credit growth is important. It demonstrates why “more liquidity” does not equal “more lending” and why lower rates do not guarantee a credit boom. The 2025/26 picture is consistent with a system in which funding is available but credit absorption is constrained.

That is a useful warning against a simplistic NEPSE model:

> **Liquidity can be abundant while credit demand, investment and equity-market breadth remain weak.**

## 1.9 Policy rate cuts and hikes: a decision tree

| Event | Possible first-order effect | Possible second-order interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Policy-rate cut | Lower short-term funding cost | Supportive easing, or response to weak demand |
| Policy-rate hike | Higher funding and discount rate | Anti-inflation action, reserve defence or financial-stability response |
| CRR reduction | More immediately deployable liquidity | Helpful only if capital, demand and risk appetite allow lending |
| SLR reduction | More flexibility in liquid-asset allocation | May improve deployment but can alter liquidity resilience |
| Deposit-collection operation | Absorbs surplus liquidity | May help corridor control without signalling a rate hike |
| Repo operation | Adds short-term liquidity | Temporary support, not proof of durable credit demand |
| Margin rule tightening | Reduces leverage against shares | Can pressure turnover and collateral values quickly |
| CD-ratio relaxation | Removes a binding constraint for some BFIs | Useful only where deposits, capital and borrowers support expansion |

Always ask whether the change is:

- a signal;
- a quantity adjustment;
- a regulatory constraint;
- a response to stress;
- temporary or structural.

## 1.10 How monetary policy reaches NEPSE

The main equity channels are:

### Discount-rate channel
Lower rates can reduce the discount rate used in valuation models. This may raise the present value of future cash flows, especially for long-duration growth assets. The effect is not uniform: higher inflation or higher risk premiums can offset it.

### Relative-yield channel
When deposit and bond yields fall, equities may appear relatively more attractive. This is a portfolio-allocation effect, not proof that shares are cheap.

### Bank-earnings channel
Rates, credit growth, deposit costs, loan yields, fees and provisions influence bank earnings. A rate cut can help borrowers and asset quality but compress margins if lending rates fall faster than funding costs or if loan demand is weak.

### Liquidity and leverage channel
Available liquidity and share-backed lending can affect turnover and risk appetite. The size of this channel must be measured; it should not be inferred from the existence of margin lending alone.

### Macro-earnings channel
Rates influence consumption, investment, property, imports and working capital. The impact on each listed company depends on its debt, pricing power, currency exposure and demand.

### Expectations channel
Markets may react before the policy change if it was anticipated, or barely react if it was fully priced in. A surprise matters more than the headline alone.

## 1.11 A monthly monetary-policy dashboard

Record the following in a spreadsheet:

| Category | Monthly observation | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Policy | SDF, policy, SLF and bank rates | Did the stance change? |
| Operations | Repo, reverse repo, deposit collection, OMO amounts | Is NRB adding or absorbing liquidity? |
| Market rate | WAIBR and corridor position | Is the operating target being achieved? |
| Funding | Deposit growth and composition | Are deposits stable and what type? |
| Credit | Private-sector credit stock, flow and YoY growth | Is lending accelerating? |
| Demand | Approvals, disbursement and sector allocation | Is credit being absorbed? |
| Risk | Gross/net NPL, provisions and capital | Is expansion creating fragility? |
| External | Remittances, imports, reserves and current account | Is the peg and external buffer comfortable? |
| Market | NEPSE returns, turnover, breadth and valuation | Has the market already priced the signal? |

Use three columns beside every number:

- source and table/page;
- publication date and data-as-of date;
- confidence and possible limitation.

## 1.12 Common analytical mistakes

1. **Treating the policy rate as a loan rate.** Borrowers pay product-specific rates, spreads and fees.
2. **Assuming a rate cut guarantees credit growth.** Demand and bank risk appetite may be the binding constraint.
3. **Confusing deposits with lendable funds.** Deposits can rise while credit stays weak.
4. **Treating the CD ceiling as the current CD ratio.** A regulatory maximum is not actual utilisation.
5. **Using nominal credit without inflation context.** Real credit growth may be materially different.
6. **Reading only the announcement.** Implementation circulars and actual operations matter.
7. **Assuming the market response is contemporaneous.** Expectations may lead; earnings may lag.
8. **Ignoring the external sector.** The peg, imports, reserves and India matter to Nepal’s policy space.
9. **Using one bank as the whole banking system.** Bank size, funding mix and asset quality differ.
10. **Backfitting a story after the market moved.** Define the signal and publication date before measuring its result.
11. **Treating a corridor-floor WAIBR as bullish by itself.** It may mean surplus liquidity and weak credit demand.
12. **Using stale rules and rates.** Label every historical instrument with its effective date.

## 1.13 A disciplined interpretation framework

Before drawing an investment conclusion, answer these questions:

1. What exactly changed?
2. Was the change expected?
3. Is it a rate, liquidity, regulatory or communication change?
4. Which institution is affected first?
5. Is the transmission supply-led or demand-led?
6. What data would confirm transmission?
7. What data would falsify the thesis?
8. How long could the effect take?
9. What is already priced into NEPSE?
10. What company-specific risks dominate the macro signal?

Only after answering those questions should monetary policy enter a company valuation or portfolio-risk assessment.

## Conclusion

NRB monetary policy is best understood as a system of operating targets, interest-rate corridors, liquidity operations, reserve requirements, macroprudential rules and external-sector constraints. The policy rate is important, but it is only one instrument in a wider framework.

For a NEPSE investor, the practical lesson is:

> **Read the policy statement for intent, the NRB operations for implementation, the banking data for transmission, company reports for earnings impact, and market valuation for what is already priced.**

That sequence prevents the most common error: treating a central-bank announcement as a guaranteed market forecast.

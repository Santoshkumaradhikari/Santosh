# Part 0 · Chapter 0.1: Nepal’s Credit Cycle: The Hidden Equity Cycle

**First published 21 Aug 2026 · Last verified 31 Aug 2026**

> **Scope note:** This chapter presents an evidence-informed framework, not a mechanical trading rule. The relationships described below are hypotheses to test against current NRB, NEPSE and company data. They are not forecasts or investment recommendations.

## Why this chapter comes first

A share price is influenced by company-specific information, valuation, earnings, investor expectations and market liquidity. In Nepal, those forces operate inside a financial system in which banks, remittances, regulation and interest rates are unusually important. That makes the credit cycle a useful starting point—but not a complete explanation of NEPSE.

The central question is not “Does credit growth always make stocks rise?” It is:

> **Through which channels, with what delay, and under what conditions can changes in credit and liquidity affect bank earnings, company cash flows and equity valuations?**

That wording matters. It separates a useful causal hypothesis from an unsupported claim that one variable explains every market move.

## Core thesis

> **Credit and liquidity are important common drivers of Nepal’s equity market. NRB strongly influences the monetary and regulatory environment, but the transmission from policy to credit, earnings and prices also depends on borrower demand, bank behaviour, asset quality, remittances, economic activity, valuation and investor expectations.**

A disciplined investor therefore treats credit data as **context and an early-warning input**, not as a standalone buy or sell signal.

## 0.1.1 — Credit growth and NEPSE: association, mechanism and limits

### The proposed transmission chain

```text
NRB policy and liquidity operations
              ↓
Funding conditions, deposit growth and bank incentives
              ↓
Private-sector credit, interest rates and credit allocation
              ↓
Household/business activity and bank asset quality
              ↓
Earnings expectations, risk appetite and valuation
              ↓
NEPSE prices and turnover
```

The chain can run in both directions. Rising share prices may improve collateral values and confidence, which can support borrowing. Falling prices can reduce collateral values and risk appetite. This feedback is why the market may move more sharply than the initial policy change would imply.

### Why the relationship is plausible in Nepal

1. **Financial-sector weight.** Banks and other financial institutions have historically represented a large share of listed-market capitalisation and turnover, although hydropower and other sectors have grown. A banking-cycle shock can therefore affect both the index and investor sentiment.
2. **Bank-centred financing.** Many households and businesses depend on bank credit for property, working capital and investment. Credit conditions can therefore affect demand and cash flow outside the banking sector.
3. **A relatively concentrated market.** Fewer instruments for hedging and a large retail presence can make risk appetite and liquidity important during both advances and corrections. This should be measured, not assumed.
4. **Collateral feedback.** Lending against securities can amplify price moves when falling collateral values trigger calls or voluntary deleveraging. The size of this channel should be tracked separately from total private-sector credit.

### What the evidence must show

A line chart with two rising or falling series is not proof of a predictive relationship. A proper test should define:

- **Credit:** claims on the private sector or BFI private-sector credit; nominal or real; commercial banks or all BFIs.
- **Market variable:** NEPSE return, not only the index level; monthly or quarterly frequency.
- **Timing:** contemporaneous, 3-, 6-, 9- and 12-month lags.
- **Controls:** policy rates, inflation, remittances, foreign-exchange reserves, GDP, turnover, political events and global risk.
- **Robustness:** different sample periods, crisis periods, sector indices and out-of-sample tests.

Use changes and returns as well as levels. Comparing a trending index level with a trending credit series can produce spurious correlation.

### Working phase map—not a law

| Observed combination | Possible interpretation | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Credit accelerating, deposits growing, interbank rate contained | Expansion may be broadening | Real credit, loan demand, valuation and sector allocation |
| Credit remains high but decelerates, CD ratio rises, funding costs increase | Late expansion or tightening risk | Margin loans, bank spreads and policy communication |
| Credit weak, deposits accumulate, rates ease | Weak demand or early recovery | Loan approvals, disbursement, NPLs and business confidence |
| Credit recovers, NPLs still rise | Recovery may be fragile | Restructuring, provisioning and recoveries |

These are **investigation prompts**, not automatic portfolio instructions. Thresholds such as 10%, 15% or 20% should be treated as working hypotheses until back-tested.

### Causality warning

Credit may affect equity prices, but the reverse can also occur, and both may respond to a third factor. The chapter should therefore use “transmission channel”, “association” and “testable causal hypothesis” unless a documented econometric study establishes stronger causality.

## 0.1.2 — Remittance → deposits → credit: an important, imperfect chain

Remittances are a major source of foreign exchange and household income in Nepal. NRB reports workers’ remittance inflows, deposits and private-sector credit in its macroeconomic publications. Recent NRB reporting also shows that remittance dependence is substantial, while credit growth and deposit growth can diverge materially. [NRB current macroeconomic reports](https://www.nrb.org.np/red/) [NRB official indicators](https://www.nrb.org.np/)

### The realistic transmission chain

```text
Foreign employment and remittance receipts
              ↓
Household income, consumption, repayment and saving
              ↓
Deposits and foreign-exchange reserves
              ↓
Bank liquidity, funding mix and risk appetite
              ↓
Credit supply × borrower demand
              ↓
Economic activity, earnings and market sentiment
```

A remittance receipt does not automatically become a new loan. It may be consumed, used to repay existing debt, invested in land, retained as a deposit, sent through an informal channel, or converted into another asset. Banks may also have deposits but limited willingness to lend.

### Do not use a textbook multiplier mechanically

CRR and SLR constrain bank liquidity, but they do not determine lending capacity by themselves. Actual lending also depends on:

- CD-ratio rules and their precise regulatory definition;
- capital adequacy and risk-weighted assets;
- liquidity and maturity requirements;
- borrower demand and creditworthiness;
- bank risk appetite and underwriting;
- loan pricing and competition;
- sectoral limits and directed lending;
- existing NPLs, restructuring and provisioning;
- the composition and stability of deposits.

It is therefore misleading to say that an NPR 100 deposit mechanically produces NPR 80–86 of lending. The correct educational statement is:

> **Reserve and liquidity rules constrain deployment of deposits, but the amount of new credit created is an outcome of regulation, capital, liquidity, bank behaviour and demand—not a fixed money-multiplier percentage.**

### A better monitoring table

| Variable | Why it matters | Minimum definition to record |
|---|---|---|
| Workers’ remittances | Foreign income and household liquidity | NPR, period, YoY change, source |
| Deposit growth | Available bank funding | All BFIs or commercial banks; nominal/real |
| Private-sector credit | Credit actually deployed | Stock, flow and YoY growth |
| CD ratio | Credit relative to deposits | Institution/system, denominator and date |
| Interbank rate | Short-term liquidity price | Weighted average and policy corridor |
| FX reserves | External-sector buffer | Import-cover basis and reporting date |

Always record the publication date. FY 2080/81, FY 2081/82 and later years must not be mixed without clearly labelling the Nepali and Gregorian fiscal years.

### Seasonality is a hypothesis to test

Festival-related remittance patterns may be real, but monthly seasonality should be estimated using several years of data and adjusted for calendar effects. Do not assume that a month is “normally quiet” from a single cycle. Test seasonal averages, year-on-year changes and seasonal adjustment before using them in a market framework.

## 0.1.3 — Credit, bank earnings and the lag problem

Credit does not translate into EPS on a fixed timetable. A possible sequence is:

1. NRB changes a policy or macroprudential instrument.
2. Banks adjust pricing, underwriting and liquidity management.
3. Approved loans are disbursed, sometimes after a substantial delay.
4. Interest income and funding costs reprice at different speeds.
5. Fees, operating costs, provisions and recoveries affect reported profit.
6. Quarterly results become public.
7. Investors revise expectations before or after the report.

NRB research indicates that monetary-policy pass-through can extend over several months, but a policy-rate lag is not the same thing as a universal EPS lag for every bank. [NRB Working Paper No. 61](https://www.nrb.org.np/contents/uploads/2025/08/nrb-wp-61-2025-08.pdf)

### What determines the bank-earnings response

Credit growth is only one input. Analyse at least:

- loan growth and mix;
- lending yields and deposit costs;
- NIM or net interest spread;
- CASA and term-deposit composition;
- fee and commission income;
- treasury gains or losses;
- operating expenses;
- gross and net NPLs;
- provision coverage, recoveries and write-offs;
- capital adequacy and regulatory changes.

Use “illustrative lag” rather than “the 6–12 month rule”. A bank with floating-rate assets, short-term deposits and weak demand can respond very differently from a bank with long-term loans, expensive deposits and rising provisions.

### A useful research design

For each policy or credit shock, create an event table with:

- announcement date;
- affected instrument;
- expected transmission channel;
- monthly credit and deposit data;
- bank-level NIM, loan growth and NPL data;
- EPS and price reaction;
- alternative explanations;
- conclusion and confidence level.

That is more reliable than claiming that every policy change produces the same three-, six- or twelve-month outcome.

## 0.1.4 — Credit contraction, asset quality and market risk

Rapid credit growth can increase later asset-quality risk, especially when underwriting weakens, collateral prices rise faster than income, or lending becomes concentrated in cyclical sectors. But NPL deterioration is not inevitable and is not caused by tightening alone. It depends on borrower cash flow, restructuring, collateral recovery, provisioning rules, management quality and the wider economy.

### The more accurate risk chain

```text
Tighter conditions or weaker activity
              ↓
Debt service becomes harder for some borrowers
              ↓
Overdue loans, restructuring and NPL classification
              ↓
Provisioning, recoveries and capital pressure
              ↓
Lower profit or ROE; possible valuation de-rating
              ↓
Bank and market repricing
```

Do not apply a universal “one percentage point NPL increase equals X percentage points of provisions” rule. Required provisions depend on loan classification, collateral, coverage and NRB rules. Analyse gross NPL, net NPL, provision coverage, restructured loans and write-offs separately.

### The 2021–2022 correction as a case study

The correction is a useful case study of how liquidity, regulation, leverage, sentiment and valuation can interact. NRB’s 2021/22 policy introduced a maximum 90% CD-ratio requirement, and subsequent policy documents addressed lending against share collateral. [NRB Monetary Policy 2021/22](https://www.nrb.org.np/contents/uploads/2021/10/Monetary-Policy-2021-22.pdf) [NRB Monetary Policy 2022/23](https://www.nrb.org.np/ofg/monetary-policy-in-english-2022-23-full-text/)

The case should be documented with a dated dataset rather than a simplified story. Include:

- NEPSE closing levels and drawdown;
- private-sector credit growth;
- deposits and CD ratio;
- policy, deposit and lending rates;
- margin-loan rules and balances, where available;
- bank NPL, provisions and EPS;
- the dates when each piece of information became public.

The lesson is not “credit predicts every crash”. It is:

> **When leverage, valuation, liquidity constraints and deteriorating expectations converge, a market can reprice faster than company fundamentals appear to change.**

## 0.1.5 — A three-number starting dashboard

Three indicators are a useful monthly entry point, not a complete model.

### 1. Private-sector credit growth

Record the definition, frequency and reporting date. Separate nominal from real growth. Compare the current rate with its own history rather than applying universal labels such as “above 20% means sell”. Also inspect the flow of new credit and sector allocation.

### 2. CD ratio

Record whether the figure is for commercial banks, all BFIs or one institution. A 90% regulatory ceiling is not the same as a 90% system average, and the distance to a ceiling is not identical to available lending demand. NRB’s published indicators have recently shown system CD ratios materially below the 90% ceiling, demonstrating why the actual current reading must be checked rather than assumed. [NRB official indicators](https://www.nrb.org.np/)

### 3. Weighted-average interbank rate

Compare it with the relevant policy corridor and observe persistence, not just a one-day spike. A low interbank rate can indicate abundant liquidity, but it does not prove that borrowers want loans or that equities are undervalued.

### Add these confirmation checks

Before drawing a market conclusion, review:

- deposit growth and remittances;
- lending and deposit rates;
- inflation and FX reserves;
- credit-to-GDP and real credit growth;
- bank NPLs, provisions and capital;
- sector credit allocation;
- NEPSE valuation, turnover and breadth;
- margin lending and collateral risk, if reliable data is available.

### Monthly worksheet

| Question | Observation | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is credit accelerating or decelerating? |  | NRB series and definition | Low / medium / high |
| Is liquidity easing or tightening? |  | Interbank rate, deposits, CD ratio | Low / medium / high |
| Is demand absorbing available liquidity? |  | Loan growth, approvals, utilisation | Low / medium / high |
| Is asset quality improving? |  | Gross/net NPL, provisions, restructuring | Low / medium / high |
| Is the market already pricing the story? |  | Valuation, breadth, turnover, prices | Low / medium / high |
| What could falsify the thesis? |  | Contradictory data or event | Low / medium / high |

The final question is essential. A framework becomes analysis only when it states what evidence would prove it wrong.

## Conclusion

Nepal’s credit cycle is a valuable lens because it connects NRB policy, deposits, lending, bank earnings, household balance sheets and equity-market liquidity. It is not a single-variable explanation of NEPSE and it is not a guaranteed timing system.

Use it as a disciplined sequence:

1. Define the data precisely.
2. Read the current NRB release, not an old threshold.
3. Separate liquidity from credit demand.
4. Separate association from causality.
5. Track the lag without assuming a fixed timetable.
6. Test bank earnings and asset quality directly.
7. Compare the macro signal with valuation and market pricing.
8. Record uncertainty and the evidence that would falsify the view.

That approach is slower than following a slogan, but it is more portable, more auditable and more useful across Nepal’s changing financial cycles.

> *A market is not an abstraction. It is a collection of human beings and institutions interacting through the price auction.*

The character of any stock exchange — its volatility, its informational efficiency, its susceptibility to manipulation and euphoria, and its capacity for rational price correction — is a direct reflection of the specific mix of participants who trade within it.

NEPSE's participant structure is unusual by global standards. It is one of the most **retail-dominated** equity markets in Asia. Its institutional investor base is thin and structurally constrained by conservative mandates. Its mutual fund sector manages a modest fraction of national financial savings. Its broker network, though recently expanded from the historical 50-broker limit to over 90+ licensed firms, remains predominantly execution-focused. Foreign institutional participation is legally restricted and practically minimal. And it has **no designated market makers** — a foundational structural absence with profound consequences for order book depth and price continuity.

This chapter maps NEPSE's participant landscape in full: who trades, what drives their behavior, how they interact, and what their composition means for positioning an investment portfolio intelligently.

## Lesson 9.1 — Retail Investors: The Dominant Force and the Structural Consequences

### The Scale of Retail Dominance

In developed equity markets, retail investors trading personal savings typically account for only 15–25% of daily turnover. The remaining 75–85% is driven by institutional capital: pension funds, mutual funds, insurance portfolios, algorithmic market makers, and proprietary trading desks.

NEPSE inverts this structure completely:
- **Retail Investors:** Account for an estimated **80–85% of daily trading volume**.
- **Institutional Participants:** Account for the remaining **15–20%**.

```
                           NEPSE DAILY TURNOVER PARTICIPATION
                 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                 │  Retail Individual Investors                      80 – 85% │
                 │  Institutional Investors (CIT, EPF, Insurers, MFs)15 – 20% │
                 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Nepal's stock market is, in the most literal sense, a people's market. Its prices are set primarily by the aggregated decisions of hundreds of thousands of individual Nepali citizens, trading via mobile apps through broker TMS portals.

### The Behavioral Profile of the NEPSE Retail Investor

Understanding the systematic behavioral tendencies of NEPSE's retail base provides actionable analytical intelligence:

```
                              RETAIL BEHAVIORAL PATTERNS
   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
   │    Herding & FOMO     │   │     Recency Bias      │   │  Bonus Share Fixation │
   │ Social media momentum │   │ Extrapolating recent  │   │ Confusing capital     │
   │ drives aggressive     │   │ bull/bear runs into   │   │ restructuring with    │
   │ multi-day cascades.   │   │ permanent conditions. │   │ intrinsic cash return.│
   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘
```

1. **Herding and Social Media Amplification:** Retail participants exhibit pronounced herding behavior. When a stock starts climbing, discussion across Facebook groups, Telegram channels, and YouTube feeds accelerates. Buying pressure creates a self-fulfilling momentum spiral. The reversal is equally abrupt: falling prices trigger stop-losses and margin calls, producing forced selling cascades.
2. **Recency Bias:** Investors systematically extrapolate recent market performance. Cycle-peak entrants treat temporary liquidity spikes as the baseline for future returns, while trough participants anchor to recent losses and remain under-invested during early accumulation phases.
3. **Bonus Share Fixation:** A unique characteristic of Nepali retail psychology is the obsession with bonus share declarations relative to underlying earnings quality. Investors often bid up share prices ahead of bonus announcements, overlooking that a bonus share issue merely capitalizes reserves without altering the company's intrinsic economic value per rupee of equity.
4. **Margin Debt Over-Reliance:** Retail traders frequently borrow against their equity portfolios using margin lending. While leverage boosts returns in bull markets, it creates severe forced-selling pressure when share values drop below required Loan-to-Value (LTV) maintenance levels.
5. **IPO Lottery Mentality:** Consistent listing premiums on initial offerings have conditioned retail investors to treat IPOs as guaranteed lottery tickets, resulting in 50x–150x oversubscriptions regardless of the issuer's balance sheet strength.

## Lesson 9.2 — Institutional Investors: CIT, EPF, Insurance Companies — Who They Are and How They Move the Market

### The Institutional Landscape

Institutional investors in Nepal hold substantial long-term capital, but their active secondary market footprint is constrained by statutory mandates and conservative asset-liability frameworks.

```
                            INSTITUTIONAL INVESTOR LANDSCAPE
        ┌────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────┬────────────────────────┐
        │ Institution                    │ Category         │ Primary Equity Focus   │
        ├────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────┼────────────────────────┤
        │ Citizen Investment Trust (CIT) │ Statutory Trust  │ Large-cap BFIs & Hydro │
        │ Employees Provident Fund (EPF) │ Statutory Pension│ Minimal (Debt/FD focus)│
        │ Life Insurance Companies       │ Insurance Float  │ Blue-chip BFIs & Hydro │
        │ Non-Life Insurance Companies   │ Insurance Float  │ Diversified Large-caps │
        │ Mutual Fund Schemes            │ Pooled Schemes   │ Broad Listed Universe  │
        └────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────┴────────────────────────┘
```

#### 1. Citizen Investment Trust (CIT - नागरिक लगानी कोष)
Established in 1991 under the Citizen Investment Trust Act 2047, CIT manages voluntary retirement schemes, gratuity funds, and unit schemes. 
- **Mandate & Strategy:** Operates a conservative mandate prioritizing government securities and bank fixed deposits, with a measured equity allocation concentrated in Class 'A' commercial banks and select infrastructure entities.
- **Market Impact:** Acts as an anchor buyer in high-quality commercial banks during deep market troughs, providing a valuation floor.

#### 2. Employees Provident Fund (EPF - कर्मचारी सञ्चय कोष)
Nepal's largest retirement fund, managing hundreds of billions of rupees in statutory contributions from civil servants, military personnel, and formal private-sector employees.
- **Mandate & Strategy:** Structurally risk-averse. The vast majority of its assets are deployed in government bonds, fixed deposits, and direct infrastructure debt (e.g., Upper Tamakoshi and other mega hydropower projects). Its direct equity participation on NEPSE represents a small fraction of total AUM.

#### 3. Life & Non-Life Insurance Companies
Regulated by the **Nepal Insurance Authority (Nepal Bima Pradhikaran)** under the *Insurance Act, 2079 (2022)*.
- **Mandate & Strategy:** Insurers manage policyholder reserves and must adhere to statutory investment limits (allocating minimum quotas to government bonds and capped percentages to listed equities).
- **Market Impact:** Life insurance companies, with long-duration liabilities, are steady buyers of dividend-yielding commercial bank and operational hydropower stocks, often rebalancing near the end of the fiscal year in Ashadh (mid-July).

## Lesson 9.3 — Mutual Funds in Nepal: Structure, NAV, Available Schemes, and Their Limited Market Depth

### The Mutual Fund Ecosystem

Mutual funds in Nepal operate under the **Securities Act, 2063** and the **Mutual Fund Regulation, 2067 (2010 AD)**. The sector encompasses approximately **35–45 registered schemes** managed by licensed Fund Management Companies (FMCs) with combined Assets Under Management (AUM) of approximately **NPR 35–50 billion**.

```
                                MUTUAL FUND STRUCTURES
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │          Closed-End Schemes             │   │            Open-End Schemes             │
   │ • Fixed capital raised via IPO.         │   │ • Units bought/redeemed directly at NAV.│
   │ • Listed and traded on NEPSE.           │   │ • Price strictly equals current NAV.    │
   │ • Frequently trade at 15–30% NAV discount│   │ • No exchange-traded discount/premium.  │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────┘   └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Discount-to-NAV Phenomenon

A persistent structural anomaly on NEPSE is that **closed-end mutual funds routinely trade at a 15% to 30% discount to their Net Asset Value (NAV)**. For example, a closed-end fund with a reported weekly NAV of NPR 12.50 per unit may trade on NEPSE at NPR 9.50.

**Why this occurs:**
- **Retail Perception:** Retail investors often treat closed-end fund units like speculative common stocks rather than transparent portfolios of underlying assets, discounting them due to low perceived trading beta and modest capital distribution yields.
- **Analytical Opportunity:** For patient value investors, buying quality closed-end funds at deep NAV discounts provides exposure to a diversified basket of blue-chip equities at a built-in margin of safety.

## Lesson 9.4 — Licensed Brokers: Broker Expansion, Tiered Licensing, and the Commission Structure

### The Historic Shift from 50 to 90+ Brokers

For decades, NEPSE operated under a restrictive **50-broker limit**, which concentrated trading access in Kathmandu and created long queues for onboarding.

Starting in 2023, SEBON implemented major market reforms, issuing licenses across three distinct tiers:
1. **Limited Operating Brokers:** Authorized to execute buy and sell orders for clients.
2. **Full Operating Brokers:** Authorized to execute trades, offer margin lending, and provide portfolio management services.
3. **Stock Dealers:** Authorized to trade on proprietary accounts and act as institutional dealers (e.g., Nagarik Stock Dealer, Nabil Stock Dealer).

Today, NEPSE operates through a network of **over 90+ licensed brokerage firms** (with broker IDs numbered into the 90s), significantly expanding regional coverage across provincial cities (Pokhara, Biratnagar, Butwal, Narayanghat, Dhangadhi, Nepalgunj).

### Current Tiered Commission Structure (Equity)

Broker commission rates are strictly regulated by SEBON. Following successive revisions (including the May 2024 / Jestha 2081 10% reduction), the current equity commission slabs are:

```
                            EQUITY BROKER COMMISSION RATES
   ┌───────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ Transaction Slab (NPR)                │ Maximum Regulated Commission Rate   │
   ├───────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────┤
   │ Up to Rs. 50,000                      │ 0.36% (or minimum Rs. 10 per trade) │
   │ Rs. 50,001 to Rs. 5,00,000            │ 0.33%                               │
   │ Rs. 5,00,001 to Rs. 20,00,000         │ 0.306%                              │
   │ Rs. 20,00,001 to Rs. 1,00,00,000      │ 0.27%                               │
   │ Above Rs. 1,00,00,000                 │ 0.243%                              │
   └───────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

*Additional Transaction Costs:*
- **SEBON Regulatory Fee:** `0.015%` of transaction value on both buy and sell sides.
- **DP Transaction Fee:** Flat `Rs. 25` per company scrip settled.
- **Capital Gains Tax (CGT):** Withheld at source by CDSC on realized profits (`7.5%` for individual holdings $\le$ 365 days; `5.0%` for individual holdings $>$ 365 days).

## Lesson 9.5 — FII Restrictions, Allowed Limits, and Their Minimal Presence in NEPSE

### Legal Framework for Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs)

Under SEBON's **Foreign Investment in Securities Regulation, 2075 (2018 AD)**, foreign institutional funds may legally invest in listed equities, debentures, and mutual funds up to a **30% aggregate foreign ownership ceiling** per company (with lower caps in banking and media).

### The Capital Account Friction

Despite legal provisions, actual foreign institutional participation on NEPSE remains **virtually zero**. The fundamental barrier is Nepal's **non-convertible capital account**:
- International fund managers require immediate, friction-free repatriation of capital and dividends to meet client redemptions.
- Multi-step documentation requirements through Nepal Rastra Bank for foreign exchange conversion and repatriation create significant operational drag.
- As a result, NEPSE remains an almost entirely domestic, closed capital market insulated from direct global fund flows.

## Lesson 9.6 — NRN and Migrant Worker Investors: Rules, Remittance Quotas, and Repatriation Rights

### The Remittance Pillar

With millions of Nepalis working abroad generating remittances exceeding 25% of national GDP, the diaspora represents a vital capital conduit for NEPSE.

```
                              DIASPORA CAPITAL CHANNELS
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │     10% Migrant Worker IPO Quota        │   │          NRN Investment Scheme          │
   │ Reserved strictly for Nepalis working   │   │ Dedicated foreign-currency accounts     │
   │ abroad with formal labor permits.       │   │ with statutory profit repatriation      │
   │ Significantly higher allotment odds.    │   │ rights under NRB exchange guidelines.   │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────┘   └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Mandatory 10% IPO Reservation:** Under SEBON regulations, every public issue must allocate **10% of total public shares to Nepalis employed abroad under formal labor permits** via formal remittance banking channels.
2. **Non-Resident Nepali Investment Accounts (NRNIA):** NRNs holding foreign citizenship or permanent residency can establish dedicated NRN investment accounts in domestic commercial banks.
3. **Repatriation Rights:** Principal capital, dividends, and post-tax capital gains can be repatriated in foreign currency through authorized banking channels upon submitting tax clearance certificates.

## Lesson 9.7 — Promoter Shareholders vs. Public Shareholders: Lock-In Rules and Power Asymmetry

### The Structural Shareholder Divide

Listed companies in Nepal feature a defined legal separation between **Promoter Shareholders** (founders, initial strategic investors) and **Public Shareholders** (general retail and institutional investors).

```
                             PROMOTER VS PUBLIC DYNAMICS
   ┌───────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ Feature                               │ Promoter / Public Reality           │
   ├───────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────┤
   │ Standard Equity Ratio (BFIs)          │ 51% Promoter / 49% Public           │
   │ Statutory Lock-In Period              │ 3 Years from date of listing/allot. │
   │ AGM Control                           │ Promoters hold effective control.   │
   │ Board Representation                  │ Majority promoter directors.        │
   │ Share Pledging                        │ Pledged promoter shares create risk.│
   └───────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Key Risk Factors for Public Shareholders

1. **The 3-Year Lock-In Expiration:** Promoter shares in hydropower and corporate issuers cannot be traded on the secondary market for **3 years** following commercial operation or public allotment. When this lock-in expires, large supplies of promoter shares can enter the market, creating downward price pressure.
2. **Promoter Share Pledging:** Promoters frequently pledge their locked-in shares to banks for commercial borrowing. If a promoter defaults, the lending bank liquidates the pledged shares in bulk on the secondary market, triggering sudden supply shocks.
3. **Related-Party Transactions:** Public shareholders must scrutinize annual report disclosures to ensure promoters are not routing lucrative corporate supply contracts to privately owned family subsidiaries.

## Lesson 9.8 — Market Makers: Why NEPSE Has None and the Liquidity Consequences

### What Market Making Is

A market maker is an institution obligated to continuously provide two-sided quotes (**both Bid and Ask prices**) for a security, earning the bid-ask spread while ensuring instant liquidity for other market participants.

### The Structural Void on NEPSE

**NEPSE has zero designated market makers for equity.** Every transaction relies strictly on the immediate coincidence of an independent buyer and seller.

```
                           CONSEQUENCES OF ZERO MARKET MAKERS
   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
   │  Wide Bid-Ask Spreads │   │ Price Gaps on News    │   │  Lower Circuit Traps  │
   │ Spreads widen to 2–5% │   │ Illiquid counters jump│   │ Sellers cannot exit   │
   │ in mid/small-caps.    │   │ multiple price tiers. │   │ during multi-day falls│
   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘
```

1. **Wide Bid-Ask Spreads:** In less-active counters (small-cap hydro, manufacturing), bid-ask spreads often expand to 2–5%, adding implicit transaction friction.
2. **Lower Circuit Entrapment:** During market panic, if natural buyers withdraw, a stock hits its daily lower circuit limit (**-10%**) immediately. Without a market maker contractually bound to absorb order flow, investors face multi-day exit lockouts.
3. **The Illiquidity Discount as Opportunity:** Because illiquidity suppresses prices below fundamental DCF values, disciplined long-horizon investors can accumulate cash-generating enterprises at substantial discounts to intrinsic worth.

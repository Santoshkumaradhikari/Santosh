Lesson 22.1 — Origins of Nepal's Promoter Webs

Private licensing in banking (1980s), insurance (2000s), and hydropower (1990s–present) required concentrated promoter capital, resulting in established industrial family houses holding dominant equity blocks across multiple listed sectors.

Lesson 22.2 — Mapping the Ownership Web

Forensic analysis requires auditing NFRS related-party notes, cross-referencing board directorships, inspecting promoter shareholding tables, and scrutinizing hydropower IPO offer documents.

Lesson 22.3 — The Core Conglomerate Risks

Conglomerate Risk Mechanism Impact on Retail Shareholders
Contagion Risk Distress in an unlisted group hydro project spills over to the listed bank. Reputational run and collateral impairment.
Circular Financing Bank loans recycled to fund rights issue subscriptions in affiliates. Recycled debt masquerading as fresh equity.
Double-Counted Capital Same promoter equity counted across multiple balance sheets. Overstated aggregate group financial resilience.

Lesson 22.4 — Regulatory Landscape

NRB enforces Single Obligor Limits (SOL) tied to core capital and weights interconnectedness at 30% under the Domestic Systemically Important Bank (D-SIB) framework. BAFIA amendments seek to separate industrial borrowers from bank directorships.

Lesson 22.5 — 7-Step Due-Diligence Framework

  1. Identify the ultimate promoter family or trust.
  2. Enumerate all connected listed and unlisted group entities.
  3. Audit related-party notes for intercompany loans and guarantees.
  4. Check share-pledging filings across the group.
  5. Identify the group's weakest, most leveraged entity.
  6. Stress-test contagion channels to your target company.
  7. Discount sum-of-the-parts group valuations.

Lesson 22.6 — The Diversification Illusion

Holding four stocks in different sectors (bank, insurer, hydro, manufacturer) that share the same promoter family balance sheet creates concentrated exposure to a single family empire rather than genuine portfolio diversification.
